# Lesson 12.5: Restrictions and inverse verification

Restricting a quadratic to one side of its vertex makes it one-to-one. The chosen branch fixes its inverse formula, and both compositions must be verified on their respective domains.

## Learning objectives

- Restrict quadratic domains, justify one-to-one behavior, and derive the corresponding inverse branches and sets.
- Verify both inverse compositions with explicit domain conditions and diagnose failed claims caused by missing restrictions.

## Learning outcome

Choose an invertible quadratic branch and prove that its inverse reverses the function on exactly the specified sets.

## Proficiency criteria

- Select and justify a one-to-one interval on one side of the vertex.
- Choose the root sign matching the restricted original inputs and exchange the restricted domain and attained range.
- Verify both identity compositions on their respective domains.
- Justify absolute-value simplifications from sign conditions and identify inverse claims invalidated by missing restrictions.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Restricting a quadratic to obtain an inverse** | For $f(x)=a(x-h)^2+k$ with $a\ne0$, either full branch $x\ge h$ or $x\le h$ is one-to-one. The corresponding inverses are $h+\sqrt{(x-k)/a}$ and $h-\sqrt{(x-k)/a}$. For these full branches, inverse inputs satisfy $x\ge k$ if $a>0$ and $x\le k$ if $a<0$. In every chosen interval, the inverse domain is the attained original range and its range is the restricted original domain; narrower intervals require their own endpoints. | Restrict a quadratic to an interval on one side of its vertex and justify why it is one-to-one there. Derive the matching inverse branch and exchange domain and range, preserving sign and endpoint conditions. | State the selected interval and justify one-to-one behavior. Solve using the root sign matching that interval. Give the inverse formula with its exact domain and range, including all endpoint restrictions. |
| **Verifying both compositions on their domains** | For $f:D_f\to R_f$, an inverse $g:R_f\to D_f$ satisfies $g(f(x))=x$ for every $x\in D_f$ and $f(g(y))=y$ for every $y\in R_f$. Each composition must be defined throughout its stated set. One successful direction alone does not establish the claimed inverse. In radical simplification, $\sqrt{u^2}=\lvert u\rvert$; replacing this by $u$ or $-u$ requires the appropriate sign condition. | Verify both inverse compositions as identity maps on their respective domains, checking that both compositions are defined. Identify and explain failed inverse claims caused by missing domain or sign restrictions. | Form and simplify both compositions and state each identity’s input set. Check that intermediate values are admissible. Justify absolute-value simplifications and identify the restriction or identity that fails in an invalid inverse claim. |
