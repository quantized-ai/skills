# Lesson 17.8: Evaluation of statistical reports

[Unit 17: Statistical distributions and inference](../unit.md) · [Algebra 2](../../../curriculum.md)

Statistical report evaluation traces a claim back to the study design, numerical summaries, and uncertainty that support it. A sound critique identifies a specific gap and rewrites the claim without discarding valid evidence.

## Learning objectives

- Assess whether reported claims match the study design and statistics.
- Identify misleading denominators, effect descriptions, or omitted uncertainty.
- Rewrite claims with defensible scope and qualification.

## Learning outcome

The student can distinguish supported findings from overclaims and communicate a statistically defensible version of a report.

## Proficiency criteria

To demonstrate proficiency, the student must independently satisfy all of the following criteria.

- Identify what was measured, who was studied, how data were collected, and which statistic supports the headline.
- Check causal language, population generalization, and claims about individuals against the design and analysis.
- Evaluate denominators, absolute and relative effects, displays, and uncertainty for misleading interpretations.
- Produce a revised claim that states the supported result and its specific limits.

## Concepts

### Concept U17-L08-C01: Claims, evidence, and uncertainty

**Content**

Target population; sampling/assignment; denominators; confounding; effect sizes; margins; graphics; selective reporting.

**Learning objectives**

- **U17-L08-C01-O1:** Evaluate a report’s statistical claim against its data collection and analysis.
  - Standards: CCSS HSS-IC.B.6 (direct).
- **U17-L08-C01-O2:** Rewrite an overstated claim to reflect uncertainty, design, and the population actually studied.
  - Standards: CCSS HSS-IC.B.6 (direct).

**Proficiency criteria**

- Trace a reported claim to the population, collection design, denominator, statistic, and stated uncertainty supporting it.
- Diagnose specific overclaims involving causation, representativeness, individual effects, graph scales, or absolute versus relative changes.
- Rewrite the claim to preserve supported evidence while explicitly limiting its scope and uncertainty.

**Explanation**

Trace a report from question to design to data to conclusion. Identify who was measured, how they were selected, whether treatments were assigned, what denominator supports each percentage, and whether uncertainty is reported. Check graph scales and compare absolute with relative differences. Critique specific unsupported steps; do not dismiss all evidence because a limitation exists.

**Worked examples**

1. An opt-in survey says “70% of 100 website visitors prefer A, so 70% of all residents prefer A.” The first statement describes respondents; the citywide inference lacks random sampling and coverage evidence. A defensible rewrite limits the 70% to participating visitors.
2. A report says a rate doubled from 1 in 1000 to 2 in 1000. Relative increase is 100%, but absolute increase is 1 per 1000 (0.1 percentage point). Both descriptions help readers assess size; neither alone establishes causation.

**Exercises**

1. A correlation between study time and grades is reported as proof that an extra hour causes a fixed grade increase. Critique. **Answer:** Observational confounding and selection may explain some association; causal language is unsupported.
2. A survey reports 52% ±4 percentage points. Does it establish a population majority? **Answer:** The interval 48%–56% includes 50%, so the stated uncertainty does not clearly establish it.

**Assessment**

A headline says “New method raises results for every student” after 40 volunteers were randomly assigned methods; the estimated mean improvement is 3 points with simulation-based evidence against no effect. Identify two overclaims and rewrite. **Expected:** An average effect does not imply every student benefits, and volunteers do not automatically represent all students. Rewrite: “In this randomized volunteer experiment, the new method improved average results by an estimated 3 points, with simulation evidence for a treatment effect; individual and broader-population effects remain uncertain.” **Rubric (4):** two concrete critiques tied to design/statistic (2, O1); appropriately bounded rewrite (2, O2).

Objective coverage: **U17-L08-C01-O1**, **U17-L08-C01-O2**.
