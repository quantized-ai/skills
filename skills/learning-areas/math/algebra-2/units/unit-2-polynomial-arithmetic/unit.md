# Unit 2: Polynomial arithmetic

[Algebra 2](../../curriculum.md)

Polynomial arithmetic begins with precise expression structure and develops addition, subtraction, multiplication, and identities from the distributive property. The unit separates the definition of a polynomial from its term-count classification, degree, and written order. Students connect these descriptions to reliable calculations, then explain why equivalent forms represent the same quantity. Most calculations use one variable; multivariable examples establish total degree. Real coefficients are permitted, including irrational coefficients. Factoring and division are developed in later units rather than assumed here.

## Prerequisites

Use Unit 1 for substitution, function notation, and the distinction between formulas and permitted inputs. Students also need signed-number arithmetic, operation order, the distributive property, and whole-number exponent laws from Algebra I. Begin by checking these skills because sign and exponent errors can obscure later structural reasoning.

## Main learning objectives

- Classify polynomial expressions and interpret terms, factors, and composite quantities in context. See [Lesson 2.1](lesson-1-definition-and-term-structure/lesson.md).
- Describe a simplified polynomial by its number of terms, degree, and leading information. See [Lesson 2.2](lesson-2-classification-and-degree/lesson.md).
- Translate between standard form and coefficient lists and evaluate with appropriate numerical-check limits. See [Lesson 2.3](lesson-3-standard-form-and-evaluation/lesson.md).
- Compute polynomial sums, differences, and products while justifying signs, distribution, and closure. See [Lesson 2.5](lesson-5-distributive-multiplication/lesson.md).
- Prove or disprove identities and use proven special products to establish numerical relationships. See [Lesson 2.7](lesson-7-polynomial-identities-and-equivalence/lesson.md).

## Concepts and topics

The topic sequence moves from finite sums, nonnegative variable exponents, signed terms, and contextual parts to classification and degree. Standard form, zero placeholders, and substitution prepare students for arithmetic. Addition and subtraction emphasize like terms and additive inverses; multiplication emphasizes complete distribution. Binomial squares, conjugate products, symbolic proof, and the Pythagorean-triple identity connect calculations to general relationships. The zero polynomial and restrictions inherited from original expressions receive explicit treatment.

## Lessons

- [2.1: Definition and term structure](lesson-1-definition-and-term-structure/lesson.md) — Define polynomials, separate additive and multiplicative structure, and interpret simple and composite parts using contextual quantities and units.
- [2.2: Classification and degree](lesson-2-classification-and-degree/lesson.md) — Classify simplified expressions by nonzero term count and degree while applying consistent leading-term, constant, and zero-polynomial conventions.
- [2.3: Standard form and evaluation](lesson-3-standard-form-and-evaluation/lesson.md) — Organize expressions in descending powers, preserve coefficient positions, and evaluate accurately while distinguishing numerical evidence from an identity proof.
- [2.4: Addition and subtraction](lesson-4-addition-and-subtraction/lesson.md) — Add and subtract through like-term collection and complete additive inverses, explaining closure and possible cancellation of leading terms.
- [2.5: Distributive multiplication](lesson-5-distributive-multiplication/lesson.md) — Multiply monomials and general polynomials through complete distribution, using degree and leading coefficient to detect possible errors.
- [2.6: Special products](lesson-6-special-products/lesson.md) — Distinguish doubled cross terms in binomial squares from cancelled cross terms in conjugate products and apply both identities.
- [2.7: Polynomial identities and equivalence](lesson-7-polynomial-identities-and-equivalence/lesson.md) — Establish identities symbolically, reject false claims with counterexamples, and generate numerical relationships from the Pythagorean-triple identity.
