# Lesson 8.6: Optional rational-function extensions

This optional extension uses polynomial division to analyze slant asymptotes and sign charts to solve rational inequalities. Its proficiency criteria apply only when the extension is selected; they are not required for completion of the core unit.

## Learning objectives

- Find and justify slant asymptotes, distinguishing end behavior from domain restrictions or possible crossings.
- Solve simple rational inequalities using interval signs and justified endpoint conditions.

## Learning outcome

When this optional lesson is selected, justify slant end behavior and complete rational-inequality solution sets.

## Proficiency criteria

- Identify a linear quotient by polynomial division and show that the function-minus-line difference tends to zero at both ends.
- Retain original domain exclusions and distinguish them from asymptotic behavior.
- Rewrite an inequality with a single factored rational expression compared with zero and analyze every interval.
- Include allowed zeros only for non-strict inequalities, exclude original denominator zeros, and account for a denominator’s sign before multiplying an inequality.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Slant asymptotes — optional extension** | When the numerator degree is exactly one greater than the denominator degree, polynomial division writes $f(x)=q(x)+r(x)/Q(x)$ with linear quotient $q$ and remainder of degree below that of $Q$ (or zero). Since $r(x)/Q(x)\to0$ as $x\to\pm\infty$, $y=q(x)$ is the slant asymptote. Original denominator exclusions still apply. A graph can cross the line at an allowed input where the remainder vanishes; an asymptote describes end behavior, not a universal barrier. | Find a slant asymptote by recognizing the degree relationship and performing polynomial division. Explain why the function-minus-line difference tends to zero at both ends, while retaining domain exclusions and allowing justified crossings. | Produce a linear quotient and proper rational remainder by division. Show that the difference from the quotient line tends to zero at both ends. Retain original exclusions and determine whether a claimed crossing is allowed. |
| **Rational inequalities — optional extension** | Rewrite the inequality as one factored rational expression compared with zero while retaining all original denominator exclusions. For a nonzero quotient, real zeros of the reduced numerator and all original excluded inputs split the domain into intervals of constant sign. Use factor signs or test inputs to select the intervals satisfying the comparison. Include an allowed zero for $\le$ or $\ge$, but not for a strict inequality; never include an original denominator zero. Multiplication by a variable denominator requires sign cases because a negative factor reverses the inequality. If the quotient is identically zero, a non-strict comparison with zero holds on the original domain and a strict comparison has no solution. | Solve a simple rational inequality by forming one quotient, retaining original exclusions, and constructing a sign chart or recognizing an identically zero expression. Justify endpoint inclusion and exclusion and explain why multiplication by an unknown-sign denominator requires sign cases. | Form one quotient compared with zero and record original exclusions. Analyze signs on every interval separated by allowed zeros and exclusions, or evaluate an identically zero expression directly. Include only endpoints permitted by both the comparison and original domain, and explain the sign risk in multiplying by a variable denominator. |
