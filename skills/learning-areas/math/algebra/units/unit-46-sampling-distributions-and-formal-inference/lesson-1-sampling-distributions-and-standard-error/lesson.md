# Lesson 46.1: Sampling distributions and standard error

Repeated-sample distributions describe the uncertainty of estimators rather than the spread of individual observations.

## Learning objectives

- Construct and compare theoretical and simulated sampling distributions of means and proportions.
- Assess when a normal approximation to a sampling distribution is justified.

## Proficiency criteria

- Keep population, sample, and sampling-distribution units distinct; use a fixed sample size and design, compare centers and standard errors, and explain simulation error.
- Check the sampling design and distribution conditions, distinguish population normality from a sampling approximation, and avoid treating a sample-size rule as a universal guarantee.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Means and proportions under repeated sampling** | For independent identically distributed observations with finite variance, $E(\bar X)=\mu$ and $SD(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n$. For independent Bernoulli trials, $E(\hat p)=p$ and $SD(\hat p)=\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. Finite sampling without replacement may require a finite-population correction; negligible fractions approximate independence. | Construct and compare theoretical and simulated sampling distributions of means and proportions. | Keep population, sample, and sampling-distribution units distinct; use a fixed sample size and design, compare centers and standard errors, and explain simulation error. |
| **Normal approximation and the central limit effect** | Sample means are exactly normal for independent normal observations and approximately normal for sufficiently large samples under appropriate finite-variance conditions. Large-sample proportion approximations require adequate expected successes and failures; skewness and rare events can make normal approximations poor. | Assess when a normal approximation to a sampling distribution is justified. | Check the sampling design and distribution conditions, distinguish population normality from a sampling approximation, and avoid treating a sample-size rule as a universal guarantee. |
