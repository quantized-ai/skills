## Reasoning activity

**Prompt:** Repair x(x−2)+3(2−x)=(x−2)(x+3).

**Private reasoning key:** Since 2−x=−(x−2), the product is (x−2)(x−3). Expansion gives x²−5x+6 in both the repaired expression and original.

Ask what changed between the original claim and the repaired explanation. If the response is only a guessed answer, use the corresponding concept’s targeted cue below; after feedback, collect a new independent attempt.

## Concept guidance

### Factoring a repeated polynomial expression

Curriculum reference: **Factoring a repeated polynomial expression** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Factor $5x(x-2)+3(x-2)$.

**Agent key:** $(x-2)(5x+3)$; the common binomial remains intact.

**Worked example:** Factor $4x(x-1)+7(1-x)$.

**Worked reasoning:** $1-x=-(x-1)$, so the result is $(x-1)(4x-7)$.


#### Teaching sequence

Temporarily name a repeated polynomial U and apply aU+bU=(a+b)U. Restore the complete polynomial unchanged. When appearances differ by sign, rewrite 1−x=−(x−1) first and absorb the minus into its outside multiplier. Expand the final product to check both nested and outer signs.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Are the apparent common factors equal or opposite?

If terms inside the common object are removed separately, replace the object by a box. If 1−x is treated as x−1, substitute x=0 to expose the sign difference before deriving the negative relation.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Use identical factors first, then opposite factors and longer repeated expressions. Reverse the task by expanding a known common-factor product and explaining what grouping preserves.

#### Assessment evidence

Require intact object recognition, opposite-factor signs, restored notation and full verification. Do not require needless expansion before a valid common factor can be seen.

Use the [fresh-question policy](../question-generation.md); the two calibration examples above are exposed learning material, not the default quiz. Collect the curriculum's full proficiency evidence, retain demonstrated components, and reassess unresolved cases with new tasks.

### Factoring by grouping

Curriculum reference: **Factoring by grouping** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Factor $x^3+2x^2+3x+6$.

**Agent key:** $x^2(x+2)+3(x+2)=(x+2)(x^2+3)$, complete over the rationals.

**Worked example:** Factor $x^3-2x^2-4x+8$ completely over the rationals.

**Worked reasoning:** $(x-2)(x^2-4)=(x-2)^2(x+2)$; grouping first exposes another factorable expression.


#### Teaching sequence

Split four terms into two groups that can leave a shared factor. Factor each group, retaining negative signs when needed, then factor the shared binomial from the resulting sum. Reinspect residual factors; in the worked cubic the remaining x²−4 splits again. Explain that a failed first grouping rejects that grouping, not all possible factorizations.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Do the groups leave identical factors after extraction?

If the groups produce opposites, factor −1 from one group before comparing. If grouping changes term signs, compare the grouped expression to the original before proceeding. If work stops too early, test the remaining factors for known identities.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Start with already favorable order, then reorder terms, use negative GCFs and expose a second factoring stage. Compare two valid groupings that give the same final product.

#### Assessment evidence

Assess equivalent regrouping, two distributions in reverse, complete residual factorization and expansion. A claim of irreducibility must have evidence beyond a failed arrangement.
