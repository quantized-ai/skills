## Reasoning activity

Use after the relevant basic procedure is accessible. Reveal the prompt first, let the student identify and repair the reasoning, and close with the correct explanation. In assess mode generate a new task instead of administering this exposed example.

**Prompt:** Is x⁴−16 completely factored over the reals as (x²−4)(x²+4)?

**Private reasoning key:** No: x²−4=(x−2)(x+2). The complete real factorization is (x−2)(x+2)(x²+4); the last quadratic has no real roots.

Ask what changed between the original claim and the repaired explanation. If the response is only a guessed answer, use the corresponding concept’s targeted cue below; after feedback, collect a new independent attempt.

## Concept guidance

### Factoring differences of squares

Curriculum reference: **Factoring differences of squares** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Factor $x^4-16$ completely over the rationals.

**Agent key:** $(x^2-4)(x^2+4)=(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$; the final quadratic has no rational roots.

**Worked example:** Is $9x^2+25=(3x-5)(3x+5)$?

**Worked reasoning:** No: that product is $9x^2-25$. A sum of positive squares does not fit the difference identity.


#### Teaching sequence

Identify two complete squares separated by subtraction; write their roots as A and B, then form (A−B)(A+B). Expand once to explain cancellation. Inspect both factors again: x⁴−16 gives (x²−4)(x²+4), and only the first splits over the reals. State whether real or rational factors are being sought.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Is the operation between the squares subtraction?

If a sum of squares uses the same real identity, multiply the proposed conjugates to expose the negative constant. If a coefficient's root is missed, square the proposed A explicitly. If factoring stops after one split, inspect each new factor.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Use numerical coefficients, several variables and repeated square structure. Include deliberate nonexamples and compare rational versus real stopping points.

#### Assessment evidence

Require both whole square roots, subtraction recognition, conjugate factors, repeated inspection and expansion; identify the coefficient system used to justify completion.

Use the [fresh-question policy](../question-generation.md); the two calibration examples above are exposed learning material, not the default quiz. Collect the curriculum's full proficiency evidence, retain demonstrated components, and reassess unresolved cases with new tasks.

### Perfect-square trinomials

Curriculum reference: **Perfect-square trinomials** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Factor $4x^2-12x+9$.

**Agent key:** The endpoints are $(2x)^2$ and $3^2$, and the middle is $-2(2x)(3)$, giving $(2x-3)^2$.

**Worked example:** Why is $x^2+8x+9$ not $(x+3)^2$?

**Worked reasoning:** That square has middle coefficient 6, not 8. Square endpoints alone are insufficient.


#### Teaching sequence

Recognize that square endpoints are necessary but not sufficient. Compute 2AB from their roots and compare it with the actual middle term before declaring a perfect square. Choose the sign from that middle term and re-expand. If the base is itself a reducible polynomial, factor it too while retaining its multiplicity.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** What doubled product do the endpoint roots require?

If x²+6x+4 is called (x+2)², compare the actual middle coefficient 6 with required 4. If a negative middle term yields a plus binomial, expand the cross products. If a squared factor is reported once, retain exponent 2.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Alternate true squares, near misses and missing-coefficient tasks; vary composite roots and signs. Ask for the exact coefficient that prevents a proposed square.

#### Assessment evidence

Assess square endpoints, cross-term calculation, sign, multiplicity and justified completion. A resemblance-based classification is insufficient without checking all three terms.

Use the [fresh-question policy](../question-generation.md); the two calibration examples above are exposed learning material, not the default quiz. Collect the curriculum's full proficiency evidence, retain demonstrated components, and reassess unresolved cases with new tasks.

## Decision model and graduated practice

A successful difference model is $9x^2-25=(3x-5)(3x+5)$: expansion gives $9x^2+15x-15x-25$, so the mixed terms cancel. In $x^2-2$, the real factors $(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2)$ expand to $x^2-2$; these coefficients are unavailable in rational factoring. Rejecting the difference identity for a sum is not a general irreducibility proof: sums of polynomial squares can sometimes factor.

For $x^4-8x^2+16$, identify $A=x^2,B=4$ and check $-2AB=-8x^2$ before writing $(x^2-4)^2=(x-2)^2(x+2)^2$. Each original copy contributes a copy of both factors. A learner who writes $(x-2)(x+2)$ has found the base factors but lost the square; ask what happened to the two copies before reteaching square recognition.

Cue “What are the whole endpoint roots?”; set up $(x^2-\square)^2$; then work the middle-term check $-2(x^2)(4)=-8x^2$, leaving restoration and further factoring. Fade with $x^4+6x^2+9=(x^2+\square)^2$; the key is $3$, and $x^2+3$ stays irreducible over the rationals.
