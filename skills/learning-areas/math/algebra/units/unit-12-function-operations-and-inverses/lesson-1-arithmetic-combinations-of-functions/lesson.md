# Lesson 12.1: Arithmetic combinations of functions

Arithmetic combinations apply function rules to the same input. Their domains retain the original restrictions, with quotients also excluding every zero of the divisor.

## Learning objectives

- Construct, evaluate, and interpret pointwise sums, differences, products, and quotients of functions.
- Determine combination domains and retain their restrictions after simplification.

## Learning outcome

Combine function values at a common input and justify the resulting formula, contextual meaning, and domain.

## Proficiency criteria

- Construct each arithmetic combination in the stated order using a common input; distinguish multiplication from composition.
- Interpret combined quantities with compatible units in a stated context.
- Intersect original domains and exclude divisor zeros for quotients.
- Preserve original restrictions after simplification and identify inputs at which the combination remains undefined.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sums, differences, and products** | Pointwise operations are $(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)$, $(f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x)$, and $(fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)$. Each has domain $D_f\cap D_g$, retained after simplification. Multiplication combines outputs at a common input; composition instead uses one output as another input. In context, sums and differences require compatible output units; products combine units. | Construct, evaluate, and interpret sums, differences, and products of functions, distinguishing pointwise multiplication from composition. Determine combination domains by intersecting original domains and preserve them after simplification. | Use a common admissible input and the stated operation. Interpret combined quantities and units in context. Represent the intersection domain accurately and retain every inherited restriction. |
| **Quotients of functions** | The quotient $(f/g)(x)=f(x)/g(x)$ is defined for $x\in D_f\cap D_g$ with $g(x)\ne0$. The divisor is the entire function $g$, and order matters. Cancellation preserves these restrictions; a simplified formula does not define the original quotient at a canceled zero or an originally excluded input. | Construct and evaluate the quotient of two functions in the stated order. Determine the quotient domain from both original domains and the nonzero-divisor condition, retaining restrictions after simplification. | Identify the entire divisor and construct the quotient in order. Impose both original domains and exclude every divisor zero. Retain exclusions after cancellation and distinguish an undefined quotient from its simplified formula. |
