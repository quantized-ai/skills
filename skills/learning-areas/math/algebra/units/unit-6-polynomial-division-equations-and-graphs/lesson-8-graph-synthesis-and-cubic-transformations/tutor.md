## Concept guidance

### Sketching a polynomial graph from factors

Curriculum reference: **Sketching a polynomial graph from factors** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Describe a structural sketch of $p(x)=(x+1)^2(x-2)$.

**Agent key:** It touches at $-1$, crosses at 2, has y-intercept $-2$, left tail down and right tail up. The degree gives at most two turning points, not their exact locations.

**Worked example:** Does degree 5 guarantee four turning points?

**Worked reasoning:** No. The bound is at most four; $x^5$ has none. Exact extrema require more evidence than degree.


#### Teaching sequence

Combine independently checked facts: degree and tails, real zeros/multiplicities, y-intercept and interval signs. Draw a continuous schematic consistent with these facts, including crossing or touching at each zero. Use degree−1 only as an upper bound on turning points; it does not locate or guarantee them. Mark a schematic estimate as such.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Are all zeros, signs, and tails mutually consistent?

If an extra crossing appears, check whether it invents a root. If a touch is drawn as a crossing, revisit multiplicity. If the graph is forced to have the maximum turning count, compare x⁵.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Move from factored cubics to higher-degree examples with repeated roots, then critique incompatible sketches. Require exact feature checks before using technology to refine nonexact locations.

#### Assessment evidence

Assess consistency across all known attributes and honesty about unlocated extrema. When the curriculum requires actual graphing verification, collect the actual artifact or observation separately.

### Transformations of the cubic parent

Curriculum reference: **Transformations of the cubic parent** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Map $(1,1)$ on $x^3$ to $g(x)=-2(x-3)^3+4$.

**Agent key:** The image is $(4,2)$; the center is $(3,4)$, not a maximum or minimum.

**Worked example:** Compare $2(3x)^3$ and $54x^3$.

**Worked reasoning:** They agree because cubing the inside scale contributes $3^3=27$, then the outside multiplier gives 54.


#### Teaching sequence

Use parent points on y=x³ and solve the inside input equation for their images. Transform outputs independently and locate the translated symmetry center. Explain that the center is an inflection point, not an extremum. Cubing an inside scale b contributes b³, so different parameter descriptions may represent the same polynomial.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Which coordinate rule maps the parent point?

If the center is called a maximum, compare values on either side. If inside and outside factors are simply multiplied, expand (bx)³. If point coordinates are swapped or shifts misread, check the original transformed formula.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Begin with translated cubics, then signed inside/outside scales and equivalent formulas. Ask for a transform from correspondences and for a justification of central symmetry.

#### Assessment evidence

Require valid point mappings, center and orientation, equivalence checks and the distinction between inflection and extrema. Do not claim unique recovery of redundant parameters.
