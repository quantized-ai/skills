## Lesson reasoning activity

**Purpose:** make the student test a consequential claim before accepting a procedure. Use after its relevant concept has been introduced; it is learning/practice evidence, not an unexposed assessment.

**Prompt:** Rewrite “Our 40 sampled students averaged 18 minutes, therefore everyone at the school takes 18 minutes.”

**Agent-only reasoning:** The sample mean describes those observations. A population-mean estimate requires the random-design justification and uncertainty; individual times vary. Preserve the observed mean while narrowing the claim.

Ask for the first unjustified step, invite a corrected explanation, and retain correct components of the original response. Then use a different representation or new case for independent evidence.

## Statistical reporting

Curriculum reference: **Statistical reporting** in [lesson.md](lesson.md#concepts). The activities below implement that row; the row remains authoritative for definitions and proficiency.

### Separate diagnostic

**Prompt:** A report says “mean 18.” Name three missing pieces needed even to interpret that number.

**Agent-only key:** A measured quantity and units, the population/sample, and collection/summary context are missing; variability and uncertainty are also needed for inference.

Use the response to choose where to begin the teaching sequence. A correct short diagnostic does not establish the concept’s complete proficiency.

### Worked model

**Prompt:** A random sample of 40 of a school's 800 students has mean travel time 18 minutes. Draft the core finding and identify missing reporting information.

**Agent-only worked reasoning:** “The sampled students averaged 18 minutes on the defined travel measure.” Estimating the school mean needs the sampling details, response count, spread and an uncertainty method. Report dates, units and displays; 18 is not a claim that every student takes 18 minutes. A spoken explanation must retain these limits.

Reveal the explanation in the sequence below, asking the learner to justify a consequential step before moving to the next one.

### Teaching sequence

1. Build the report from the investigative question, not from a graph chosen for appearance.
2. Pair each conclusion with the design fact and summary that warrants it.
3. Draft technical and plain-language versions side by side and check that neither removes uncertainty or widens the population.

### Practice progression

Write a caption for a supplied time distribution; then assemble question/design/data/display/uncertainty/conclusion sections from the school case; finally deliver or record a short oral explanation and answer a challenge about generalization. Text alone records written reasoning, not oral delivery.

**Construction and verification controls:** Supply short data summaries with some deliberately missing design details; require a chart suited to variable type and separate technical/plain-language accounts.

### Responsive hints and misconceptions

**First conceptual cue:** Which facts let another person reconstruct how the number was obtained?

If jargon substitutes for an explanation, ask the learner to state who was observed and what changed. If a plain-language version becomes causal, point to the assignment procedure and have them revise only that claim.

Give one relevant cue at a time and wait. If a cue does not help, use the indicated representation/setup before demonstrating the next worked step. Mark any mathematically supported attempt assisted; a later corrected response to the same example remains exposed.

### Assessment case checklist

Generate fresh tasks that collectively establish each of these curriculum obligations:

- Produce a written report and an oral explanation that make the study reproducible at the design level.
- Match displays and summaries to variable types.
- Describe uncertainty and scope, and adapt language without changing the evidence.

**Required case selection:** Design reproducibility, appropriate display and uncertainty, written report and actual oral evidence when required.

At least one independent task must transfer the reasoning to a changed representation, context, exception or reversed question. Separate numerical correctness from missing proof, assumptions, construction, reporting or technology evidence. ## Critical evaluation of published findings

Curriculum reference: **Critical evaluation of published findings** in [lesson.md](lesson.md#concepts). The activities below implement that row; the row remains authoritative for definitions and proficiency.

### Separate diagnostic

**Prompt:** A chart compares 20 successes out of 25 with 30 out of 100. Which group has the higher success rate?

**Agent-only key:** The first: 80% versus 30%; larger success count alone is misleading.

Use the response to choose where to begin the teaching sequence. A correct short diagnostic does not establish the concept’s complete proficiency.

### Worked model

**Prompt:** An advertisement says an app doubles achievement because its 12 volunteer users scored higher than nonusers. Evaluate the claim.

**Agent-only worked reasoning:** The direction and amount cannot be checked without both groups' scores, denominators and the definition of achievement. Self-selection and confounding prevent a causal conclusion from this comparison alone. This is insufficient causal evidence, not proof the app has no effect.

Reveal the explanation in the sequence below, asking the learner to justify a consequential step before moving to the next one.

### Teaching sequence

1. Reconstruct denominators and definitions before evaluating the conclusion.
2. Distinguish known contradictions from details simply omitted.
3. Match each limitation to the claim it blocks: missing random assignment blocks the proposed causal inference, whereas selective display may distort magnitude.

### Practice progression

Repair count-only headlines; then inspect a truncated-axis graphic alongside its actual values; finally rewrite the app claim with explicit limits and list the smallest additional evidence needed for its stronger claim. Include a sound report that should be accepted with its stated scope.

**Construction and verification controls:** Vary omitted denominators, truncated scales, attrition and confounding; include well-supported reports so critique does not always mean rejection.

### Responsive hints and misconceptions

**First conceptual cue:** What other differences might separate volunteers from nonusers?

If every omission is called fraud, ask whether intent is evidenced. If no causal evidence becomes “no effect,” ask whether the available design can distinguish no effect from confounding.

Give one relevant cue at a time and wait. If a cue does not help, use the indicated representation/setup before demonstrating the next worked step. Mark any mathematically supported attempt assisted; a later corrected response to the same example remains exposed.

### Assessment case checklist

- Identify the particular missing information or faulty inference.
- Distinguish unavailable evidence from known error.
- Retain only claims supported by the actual design and uncertainty.

**Required case selection:** Identify the precise information gap, repair claim language, distinguish unknown from false and association from causation.

## Repair the strength of a claim without discarding its result

A hypothetical report says “20 of 25 volunteers improved with the app, compared with 30 of 100 nonusers; the app caused a 50% improvement.” The observed improvement rates are 80% and 30%, a 50-percentage-point difference, not a 50% relative increase. Relative to 30%, the observed rate is about 167% higher. Because app use was self-selected, neither calculation establishes a causal effect. A defensible revision is: “Improvement was reported by 80% of these app volunteers and 30% of these nonusers; selection and other group differences limit causal interpretation.”

If a learner fixes the denominator but keeps “caused,” ask what determined app use. Then supply a claim/design/evidence outline; finally model one descriptive clause and leave its limitation. Fade by giving accurate percentages but an overstated population, then require a whole report independently. A valid alternative report can use counts, rates, or both if denominators and scope remain clear. A written script can be assessed as writing; oral delivery requires actual spoken evidence.
