# Lesson 53.4: Moves, countermoves, and nonmyopic analysis

Theory of moves starts from an existing outcome and evaluates the consequences of alternating strategic changes.

## Learning objectives

- Explain theory-of-moves rules and represent alternating moves and stopping choices in a game tree.
- Use backward reasoning to compare simultaneous-game and theory-of-moves predictions for two-by-two ordinal conflicts and describe extension to larger games.

- Model a literary or real-life conflict as a two-by-two strict ordinal game and compare predictions from Nash equilibrium and theory of moves.

- Identify cyclic two-by-two strict ordinal games and explain how assumptions about stopping differ from the mere existence of a closed move sequence.

## Proficiency criteria

- State the initial cell and potential first mover, preserve unilateral alternating changes, distinguish terminal from intermediate payoffs, and identify cycles explicitly.
- Track each decision-maker’s preference over terminal outcomes, analyze prisoners’ dilemma and chicken from stated initial cells, document cycle and initiation conventions, and qualify predictions when several paths remain.

- Justify each player’s rankings from the stated conflict, specify the initial cell and move conventions, analyze both initiators, and explain how model assumptions limit the prediction.

- Track whose turn follows each cell in both orientations, check whether that player has their top-ranked payoff, and distinguish a cyclic classification from a prediction of endless play.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rules of the theory of moves** | Play starts at a status-quo cell; either player may initiate a unilateral switch, then players alternate switching or stopping. Only the terminal cell pays. An initiator rejects a path ending worse or returning to the initial cell. With common knowledge of preferences, players anticipate rational responses; a profitable initiating move takes precedence over staying. | Explain theory-of-moves rules and represent alternating moves and stopping choices in a game tree. | State the initial cell and potential first mover, preserve unilateral alternating changes, distinguish terminal from intermediate payoffs, and identify cycles explicitly. |
| **Backward reasoning and comparison of predictions** | In a two-by-two game, evaluate each possible alternating cycle through its return to the initial state, then compare stopping and continuation backward; the return remains a terminal benchmark, while repeated cycling is excluded. Compare both initiators. Under the two-sidedness convention, a player may preempt another’s profitable move by inducing an outcome both prefer to the other move’s result, even if the preempting player would otherwise stay. Larger games require considering multiple paths and an explicit termination convention. | Use backward reasoning to compare simultaneous-game and theory-of-moves predictions for two-by-two ordinal conflicts and describe extension to larger games. | Track each decision-maker’s preference over terminal outcomes, analyze prisoners’ dilemma and chicken from stated initial cells, document cycle and initiation conventions, and qualify predictions when several paths remain. |
| **Strict ordinal conflict models** | A two-person conflict from literature or life can be modeled by two strategies per player and strict rankings of the four outcomes for each player. Rankings specify preferences, not interpersonal payoff differences. The initial outcome and information assumptions determine which move analysis applies; a model comparison must retain the same rankings under both analyses. | Model a literary or real-life conflict as a two-by-two strict ordinal game and compare predictions from Nash equilibrium and theory of moves. | Justify each player’s rankings from the stated conflict, specify the initial cell and move conventions, analyze both initiators, and explain how model assumptions limit the prediction. |
| **Cyclic games and stopping assumptions** | In the cyclic-game classification, an orientation around a two-by-two strict ordinal matrix is cyclic when every scheduled mover would depart from a cell giving that mover less than their best outcome. A best-ranked payoff at a mover’s turn blocks that orientation under the stop-at-best rule. This classification is stronger than merely drawing a closed sequence of legal moves. Basic theory-of-moves rational termination prevents an initiator choosing a return to the start; allowing repeated cycling or unequal moving endurance changes the rules and requires separate stopping assumptions. | Identify cyclic two-by-two strict ordinal games and explain how assumptions about stopping differ from the mere existence of a closed move sequence. | Track whose turn follows each cell in both orientations, check whether that player has their top-ranked payoff, and distinguish a cyclic classification from a prediction of endless play. |
