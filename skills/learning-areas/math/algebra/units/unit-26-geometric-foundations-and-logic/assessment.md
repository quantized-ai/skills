These original prompts and checked reasoning anchors calibrate mathematical accuracy. They are **not a fixed student quiz** and are not a complete assessment blueprint. They also serve as worked examples in the paired tutors, so exposure makes them unsuitable for independent reassessment. ## Lesson 26.1

[Curriculum](lesson-1-objects-and-measurement/lesson.md) · [Tutor](lesson-1-objects-and-measurement/tutor.md)

**Prompt:** B lies between A and C, with AB=4 and BC=7. Rays OP and OQ bound a 70° angle with OR strictly inside and angle POR=25°. Find AC and angle ROQ. Classify a square and distinguish parallel from skew lines.

**Checked reasoning:** AC=11 and angle ROQ=45° by the stated betweenness/interior conditions. A square is both a rectangle and a rhombus, hence a parallelogram; under this curriculum's exclusive trapezoid convention it is not a trapezoid. Distinct parallel lines are coplanar and disjoint; skew lines are noncoplanar. A ray has one endpoint, a segment two; a circle is a planar fixed-positive-distance locus.

**Coverage limit:** Mix object versus measure notation, degenerate incidence, angle classifications, inclusive quadrilateral categories and the declared trapezoid convention; do not infer betweenness from a sketch alone.

## Lesson 26.2

[Curriculum](lesson-2-conditional-statements/lesson.md) · [Tutor](lesson-2-conditional-statements/tutor.md)

**Prompt:** Analyze “If a quadrilateral is a square, it is a rectangle.” Give converse, inverse, contrapositive and a counterexample to any false direction.

**Checked reasoning:** Converse: if rectangle, then square; false for a 2-by-3 rectangle. Inverse: if not square, then not rectangle; the same rectangle refutes it. Contrapositive: if not rectangle, then not square; equivalent to the true original. A biconditional needs both directions. Definitions specify categories; theorem status requires proof, not many agreeing sketches.

**Coverage limit:** Include universally quantified claims, one valid counterexample, definitions/postulates/conjectures/theorems and insufficient observational evidence; retain all hypotheses when constructing a counterexample.

## Lesson 26.3

[Curriculum](lesson-3-conjecture-and-deductive-proof/lesson.md) · [Tutor](lesson-3-conjecture-and-deductive-proof/tutor.md)

**Prompt:** A student measures many parallelograms and conjectures their diagonals bisect each other. Give a coordinate proof for vertices $(0,0),(a,0),(a+b,c),(b,c)$ with a and c nonzero. Then explain what the measurements established.

**Checked reasoning:** Both diagonals have midpoint $((a+b)/2,c/2)$, establishing bisection for this general representation; the assumptions avoid degeneracy. Measurements motivated the conjecture but did not prove it for every parallelogram. A two-column or flow proof must preserve the same dependency: coordinates and midpoint definition precede equality of midpoints.

**Coverage limit:** Require actual investigations across every listed family in the curriculum (angles, congruence, triangle segments, polygon angles, diagonals, circles), then deductive arguments in each specified format/method; one coordinate example does not certify all of them.

## Lesson 26.4

[Curriculum](lesson-4-euclidean-and-spherical-geometry/lesson.md) · [Tutor](lesson-4-euclidean-and-spherical-geometry/tutor.md)

**Prompt:** On a unit sphere, use the North Pole and two equatorial points 90° apart to form a triangle of minor great-circle arcs. Compare its angle sum and lines with a Euclidean triangle.

**Checked reasoning:** Two meridians meet at the pole at 90° and each meets the equator at 90°, so sum is 270°. The triangle lies in a suitable hemisphere. Great circles are spherical lines; two distinct ones intersect at antipodal points, so no parallels. Non-equatorial latitude circles are not spherical lines.

**Coverage limit:** Vary sphere diagrams and non-antipodal endpoints, compare Euclidean/spherical assumptions, and distinguish a small circle from a great circle before making shortest-path claims.
