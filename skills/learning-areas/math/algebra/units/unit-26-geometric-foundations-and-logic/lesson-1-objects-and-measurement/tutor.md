## Concept teaching plans

### Basic objects and incidence

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** Two spatial lines do not meet. Must they be parallel?

**Private diagnostic key:** no; they may be skew if noncoplanar.

**Teach in this order:** Name objects before measures; distinguish collinearity/coplanarity; use a spatial example to test the definition of parallel.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** In a cube, a front-bottom edge and the back-right vertical edge are disjoint and noncoplanar, hence skew. Opposite edges of one square face are coplanar and disjoint, hence parallel. A segment has endpoints; its containing line extends indefinitely.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If every disjoint pair is called parallel, ask whether a plane contains both; next compare edges on one face with skew edges. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Identify points/lines/rays/segments → incidence → spatial parallel/perpendicular/skew classification. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Undefined objects versus defined subsets; endpoints; coplanarity; valid incidence premises. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Use incidence and coplanarity conditions correctly; do not call skew lines parallel or equate a segment with its length.

### Distance and angle measure

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** AB=3,BC=5. Is AC necessarily 8?

**Private diagnostic key:** only if B lies between A and C on the segment.

**Teach in this order:** State order/interior conditions; apply addition to measures; distinguish congruent objects from equal numerical lengths/angles.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** Rays OA,OB form a75° angle with OC interior and ∠AOC=28°. Angle addition gives ∠COB=47°. Without the interior-ray condition the subtraction is unjustified. A circle of radius 4 comprises all planar points distance 4 from its center, not its interior disk.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If a sketch supplies betweenness without a given, ask which premise authorizes addition; next state the missing condition explicitly. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Segment/angle addition → missing measure → distinguish circle locus and drawing assumptions. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Nonnegative distance; betweenness/interior ray; nonreflex measure; congruence versus equality; positive-radius locus. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Identify endpoints, vertices, centers, and radii; write equal measures separately from congruent-object notation; check betweenness or interior-ray conditions before adding.

### Angle and polygon classifications

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** Is a square a rectangle, rhombus and trapezoid here?

**Private diagnostic key:** rectangle/rhombus yes; trapezoid no under exactly-one-parallel-pair convention.

**Teach in this order:** Build inclusion relations; separate side and angle tests; identify simple/convex/concave and regular conditions; declare trapezoid convention.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** An equilateral triangle is isosceles because 'at least two' equal sides includes three. A rhombus is a kite under the inclusive adjacent-pairs definition, but need not be regular because its angles may differ. Classify by stated definitions rather than drawing orientation.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If an equilateral triangle is excluded from isosceles, ask whether 'at least' means 'exactly'; next count equal pairs. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Angle classes → triangle classifications → inclusive quadrilateral hierarchy and counterexamples. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Complement/supplement conditions; adjacency/vertical incidence; inclusive categories; exclusive trapezoid; regular requires equal sides and angles. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Distinguish adjacent from supplementary and regular from merely equilateral; identify a square as both a rectangle and a rhombus and state the adopted trapezoid convention.

## Extended private calibration

**Prompt:** B lies between A and C, with AB=4 and BC=7. Rays OP and OQ bound a 70° angle with OR strictly inside and angle POR=25°. Find AC and angle ROQ. Classify a square and distinguish parallel from skew lines.

**Private worked key:** AC=11 and angle ROQ=45° by the stated betweenness/interior conditions. A square is both a rectangle and a rhombus, hence a parallelogram; under this curriculum's exclusive trapezoid convention it is not a trapezoid. Distinct parallel lines are coplanar and disjoint; skew lines are noncoplanar. A ray has one endpoint, a segment two; a circle is a planar fixed-positive-distance locus.

This previously checked composite example can connect concepts after instruction. Split it into manageable turns; it does not replace the distinct diagnostic and worked model for each concept.
