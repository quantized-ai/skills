## Reasoning and error-analysis activity

**Ask:** Lines of latitude are labeled parallel spherical lines. Ask the student to locate the first invalid inference, repair the reasoning, and explain a check or counterexample.

**Private reasoning and response:** Except equator, latitude circles are not great circles. All distinct great circles meet at antipodal points; ask whether their planes pass through the sphere's center.

If the student gives only a corrected answer, ask why the original method failed. If the reason is secure, request a different example where the distinction matters. If they remain stuck, use the targeted response below for the relevant concept and mark the attempt assisted.

## Concept teaching plans

### Spherical lines and triangles

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** Are lines of latitude all spherical lines?

**Private diagnostic key:** no; only the equator among standard latitudes is a great circle.

**Teach in this order:** Identify great circles via planes through sphere center; distinguish minor arcs; compare model assumptions before transferring planar theorems.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** North Pole and equatorial longitudes 0° and 60° form a minor-arc triangle. Equatorial angles are 90° each and polar angle 60°, total 240°. Euclidean 180° fails because 'lines' and geometry differ. Distinct great circles meet at antipodal points, so there are no spherical parallels.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If a flat sketch suggests parallel meridians, ask where they meet on the sphere; next trace both to the poles. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Great/small circles → spherical triangle → shortest path and non-antipodal limitations. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Great-circle lines; no parallels; minor-arc/hemisphere setting; angle sum>180°; antipodal exception. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Distinguish great circles from other circles on the sphere and link the changed angle and parallel relationships to the geometric setting.

## Extended private calibration

**Prompt:** On a unit sphere, use the North Pole and two equatorial points 90° apart to form a triangle of minor great-circle arcs. Compare its angle sum and lines with a Euclidean triangle.

**Private worked key:** Two meridians meet at the pole at 90° and each meets the equator at 90°, so sum is 270°. The triangle lies in a suitable hemisphere. Great circles are spherical lines; two distinct ones intersect at antipodal points, so no parallels. Non-equatorial latitude circles are not spherical lines.

This previously checked composite example can connect concepts after instruction. Split it into manageable turns; it does not replace the distinct diagnostic and worked model for each concept.

## Further task construction

Vary sphere diagrams and non-antipodal endpoints, compare Euclidean/spherical assumptions, and distinguish a small circle from a great circle before making shortest-path claims.
