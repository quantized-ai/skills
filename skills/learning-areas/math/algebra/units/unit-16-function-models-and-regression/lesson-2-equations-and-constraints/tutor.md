## Concept teaching plans

### Equations from quantitative relationships

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** A trip of 120 km takes $120/v$ hours at v km/h. Set up a 3-hour trip.

**Private diagnostic key:** $120/v=3$, v>0; v=40.

**Teach in this order:** Translate a relationship using defined quantities; mark the original domain before solving; compare candidate values with the situation and label two-variable relationship axes.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** A cooling model $T=20+60(1/2)^t$ reaches 35 when $60(1/2)^t=15$, hence $(1/2)^t=1/4$, t=2. Time is nonnegative; substitution gives 35. This is a model equation, not a linear-rate claim.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If every equation is made linear, ask which operation the words imply; for repeated percentage change, show successive multiplication before writing a power. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Translate linear/rational situations → radical or exponential relation → graph a two-variable relationship and reject an infeasible candidate. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Linear, quadratic, rational, radical and exponential representations across the lesson; original domain; units; contextual root checks. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Define quantities and translate their relationships into an appropriate equation family. Represent a two-variable relationship on axes labeled with quantities, units, and scales. Solve with original restrictions intact; check candidates and reject contextually invalid values.

### Feasible sets and inequality constraints

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** Two integer counts satisfy $3x+5y\le16$. Is (2,2) feasible? Is (1/2,2)?

**Private diagnostic key:** first yes; second fails integrality.

**Teach in this order:** Translate each restriction separately; intersect half-planes; then retain only allowed lattice points. Test one boundary and one excluded point in every original condition.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** For $x+y\ge4$, $2x+y\le6$, x,y≥0 integers: subtracting inequalities implies x≤2. Enumerating x=0,1,2 gives y=4..6, 3..4, and 2 respectively: six feasible points.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If shading is treated as the answer for counts, ask whether half an item can be chosen; next mark lattice intersections inside the region. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Test membership → graph inclusive/strict boundaries → enumerate or justify integer choices. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Every simultaneous constraint; boundary inclusion; nonnegativity; integer versus continuous interpretation; a witness and a rejected point. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Translate all conditions with correct inequality directions and strict or inclusive boundaries. Include nonnegative and integer restrictions when the quantities require them. Check every condition for each candidate and justify its feasibility or identify a violated condition.

### Polynomial constraints in contextual models

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** For x>0, solve $x(x-5)\le0$.

**Private diagnostic key:** $0<x\le5$; zero is algebraically allowed but context excludes it.

**Teach in this order:** Build the polynomial model; move terms to one side; locate boundary roots; test signs between roots; intersect with the physical domain.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** A revenue target $x(10-x)\ge16$, with 0≤x≤10, gives $(x-2)(x-8)\le0$. Signs at 0,5,9 are +,−,+, so x∈[2,8]. Equality reaches the target and both endpoints are feasible.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If roots alone are returned for an inequality, ask whether x=5 reaches the target; next draw intervals split at 2 and 8. If the first prompt is insufficient, use the next indicated representation/setup; only then reveal one calculation or inference. Ask the student to finish the remaining reasoning.

**Practice progression:** Factorable boundaries → repeated roots that do not change sign → approximate roots and domain intersection. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Polynomial equation and inequality; multiplicity; sign chart; strict/inclusive endpoints; contextual domain; approximate roots labeled approximate. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Define quantities and units, preserve all real roots, analyze every sign interval with correct endpoint inclusion, and explain which algebraic solutions or intervals the modeled context excludes.

## Extended private calibration

**Prompt:** A rectangular display has width $x$ m, length $x+3$ m, and area 40 m². A separate supply has a budget of 30 credits for items costing 4 and 7 credits. Build both models and describe the feasible choices.

**Private worked key:** $x(x+3)=40$ gives $(x+8)(x-5)=0$; only $x=5$ m is a valid width and length is 8 m. For counts $u,v$, require $4u+7v\le30$, $u,v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$. $(2,3)$ costs 29 and is feasible; $(1/2,4)$ costs 30 but is not feasible for indivisible items. A boundary graph uses item counts on each axis and intercepts 7.5 and $30/7$; retain lattice points in the first-quadrant half-plane.

This previously checked composite example can connect concepts after instruction. Split it into manageable turns; it does not replace the distinct diagnostic and worked model for each concept.

## Additional worked coverage

A rectangular garden has width x meters, length 10−x meters, and requires area at least 16 m². Physical dimensions require $0<x<10$. The inequality $x(10-x)\ge16$ becomes $(x-2)(x-8)\le0$, so its sign intervals select $[2,8]$, already within the physical domain. Both endpoints are included because they give area exactly 16. A graph of area against width confirms the crossing roots and the feasible interval; numerical roots alone do not establish which intervals work.

Use this reasoning as instruction or private calibration. Generate a fresh independent counterpart after exposure; this is not a fixed reassessment task.

## Additional teaching and execution activities

### Radical and higher-degree model checks

A model gives travel time $t=\sqrt{d/5}$ hours for nonnegative distance d in kilometers, with the coefficient carrying the needed units. To reach t=3 hours, square the nonnegative relation to obtain d/5=9, so d=45 km; substitution gives3. If solving $\sqrt{x+4}=x-2$, first require x≥2. Squaring yields x²−5x=0, candidates0 and5; only5 satisfies the original. Ask why the algebraic transformation produced an inadmissible candidate.

For a contextual capacity condition $x(x-2)^2\ge0$ with0<x≤4, boundary roots are0 and2. The even root2 does not reverse sign; the square is nonnegative and x is positive, so all(0,4] is feasible, including2. For $x^3-4x\le0$, factoring x(x−2)(x+2) gives sign intervals: negative on(−∞,−2), positive on(−2,0), negative on(0,2), positive on(2,∞). The algebraic set is(−∞,−2]∪[0,2]; a positive-length domain reduces it to(0,2]. Use these to contrast a repeated-root sign chart with simple roots. When roots are obtained graphically rather than exactly, label estimates and test signs between consecutive roots; do not use a graph's pixel position as an exact endpoint.
