# Lesson 17.3: Populations, samples, and study design

Statistical conclusions depend on who was measured and how the data were generated. The lesson separates population inference, causal comparison, and specific threats from bias or confounding.

## Learning objectives

- Distinguish target populations, sampling frames, parameters, and sample statistics, including repeated-sample variation.
- Classify surveys, observational studies, and experiments and distinguish random sampling from random assignment.
- Diagnose bias or confounding, propose targeted design repairs, and state the supported population and causal scope.

## Learning outcome

The student can evaluate a study’s design and state the population and causal conclusions it can reasonably support.

## Proficiency criteria

- Identify the target population, sampling frame, fixed parameter, and statistic that varies across samples.
- Determine whether exposures were assigned and identify the sampling and assignment mechanisms.
- Match population generalization and causal conclusions to the actual design without assuming perfect balance or coverage.
- Tie each proposed repair to a specific source of bias or confounding and explain why a larger biased sample does not remove systematic error.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parameters, statistics, and random samples** | A target population is the group of interest; a sampling frame is the set from which units can be selected. A parameter describes the defined population and remains fixed across repeated samples; a statistic is calculated from a sample and varies. Probability sampling uses known selection probabilities; a simple random sample gives every subset of the specified size equal probability. Inference depends on the sampling design and adequate frame coverage, with nonresponse and measurement limitations still relevant. | Identify the target population, sampling frame, population parameter, and sample statistic, and distinguish a fixed parameter from a statistic’s repeated-sample variation. Explain how random sampling supports population inference and how frame coverage and other design limitations restrict its scope. | Identify the population, frame, parameter, and statistic separately. Explain what varies across repeated samples and what remains fixed for the defined population. Connect known random selection mechanisms to population inference and state coverage or response limitations. |
| **Surveys, observational studies, and experiments** | A survey collects responses to questions and is ordinarily an observational data-collection method. An observational study does not assign the exposure of interest; an experiment does. Experiments need not use random assignment. Random sampling supports population generalization under an adequate sampling design; random assignment makes treatment groups comparable in expectation and supports causal attribution when the experiment is well implemented. Neither mechanism substitutes for the other, and random assignment does not guarantee identical groups or population representativeness. | Classify surveys, observational studies, and experiments by data collection and exposure assignment, recognizing that a survey may be observational. Distinguish random sampling from random assignment and state the population and causal conclusions each actual design can support. | Identify whether data came from questions or other measurements and whether researchers assigned the exposure. Distinguish random selection of participants from random assignment of treatments. Qualify causal and population conclusions using the actual design, without assuming perfectly balanced groups. |
| **Bias, confounding, and design repair** | Sampling variation is chance fluctuation; bias is a systematic tendency in the data-collection or estimation process. Convenience selection, voluntary response, undercoverage, nonresponse, and leading questions can create bias. Confounding occurs when the exposure’s association with the outcome cannot be separated from another variable’s influence. A design repair must address the identified mechanism; increasing the size of the same biased sample may reduce random variation while leaving systematic error. | Identify the mechanism producing selection bias, undercoverage, nonresponse, response bias, or confounding in a study. Propose a targeted design revision, explain why larger sample size alone does not repair bias, and identify limitations that may remain. | Name the specific mechanism connecting a design weakness to a distorted conclusion. Propose a revision that addresses that mechanism and state a remaining limitation. Distinguish reduced sampling variation from reduced systematic bias. |
