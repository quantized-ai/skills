# Lesson 17.2: Normal distribution models

A normal distribution is a model whose shape must be justified before areas are interpreted. Standard scores connect original measurements to cumulative areas and estimated population percentages.

## Learning objectives

- Assess the appropriateness of a normal model from shape and context.
- Use mean and positive standard deviation to describe central normal intervals and approximate percentages.
- Standardize boundaries and use tables or technology to calculate and interpret normal areas, percentages, and expected counts.

## Learning outcome

The student can select and use a normal model with correct area calculations and explicit model-based interpretation.

## Proficiency criteria

- Justify a normal approximation using shape and contextual constraints, including whether impossible tails carry material probability.
- Locate central intervals and associate them with the approximate normal percentages.
- Convert boundaries to signed standard scores and calculate the correct tail or interval area using cumulative values, complements, or differences.
- Report percentages or expected counts as model-based estimates rather than guaranteed observed results.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Normal shape and model appropriateness** | A normal distribution is a continuous, symmetric, unimodal model with mean $\mu$, positive standard deviation $\sigma$, and total area $1$. Approximately $68\%$, $95\%$, and $99.7\%$ of its area lie within one, two, and three standard deviations of the mean. Suitability depends on shape and context: strong skew, multiple clusters, influential outliers, or substantial modeled probability for impossible values can undermine the approximation. Matching mean and standard deviation alone does not establish normality. | Judge whether a normal model is reasonable from distribution shape, outliers, and contextual constraints. Use mean and standard deviation to locate central normal intervals and interpret their approximate percentages as model-based estimates. | Evaluate normal-model suitability using symmetry, modality, unusual values, and contextual bounds. Locate intervals centered at the mean and assign the appropriate approximate normal percentages. Distinguish normal-model percentages from guarantees about an arbitrary dataset. |
| **Standard scores and normal areas** | For $\sigma>0$, $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$ expresses signed distance from the mean in standard-deviation units. The cumulative standard normal function is $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$; right-tail area is $1-\Phi(z)$ and interval area is $\Phi(z_2)-\Phi(z_1)$ for $z_1\le z_2$. Tables or technology supply these areas; output conventions and distribution parameters must match the event. Area represents a modeled population fraction; multiplying by population size gives an expected count, not a guaranteed count. | Standardize measurement boundaries and compute left-tail, right-tail, and interval normal areas using a table or correctly configured technology. Interpret a normal area as an estimated population percentage or expected count and distinguish expectation from an observed guarantee. | Use the specified mean and positive standard deviation to calculate signed standard scores. Match the stated event to cumulative areas, complements, or differences and verify the table or technology convention. Convert areas to percentages or expected counts and state the normal-model qualification. |
