# Lesson 57.2: Nonlinear systems and numerical intersections

Function intersections can be found by solving an equality of outputs or locating zeros of their difference. Algebraic and numerical methods require common domains, justified precision, and attention to intersections a graph or sign-change search can miss.

## Learning objectives

- Relate equal outputs, graph intersections, and zeros of a difference on the common domain.
- Use graphs, tables, and successive approximation to refine and verify numerical intersections.
- Identify limits of numerical evidence and classify the complete intersection set of two quadratic functions.

## Learning outcome

Find and verify function intersections, distinguish numerical evidence from exact conclusions, and justify complete solution sets for two quadratic functions.

## Proficiency criteria

- Restrict the equation and difference to the common domain and explain their equivalent solution inputs.
- Locate candidates with graphs or tables, refine them to a stated precision, and check the original outputs.
- Require continuity for sign-change bracketing; account for tangencies and distinguish a limited viewing window from proof of completeness.
- Simplify the difference of two quadratic functions, solve its actual equation, and verify the full real intersection set.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Equations as intersections and successive approximations** | On the common domain, $f(x)=g(x)$ is equivalent to an intersection of their graphs and a zero of $h(x)=f(x)-g(x)$. Graphs and tables locate candidates; successive interval refinement can bound a root when $h$ is continuous and endpoint signs are opposite. Discontinuities invalidate that guarantee. Tangent intersections may have no sign change, and a finite window may miss roots. A small output difference alone does not establish a stated input error. | Explain the equivalence of equal outputs, graph intersections, and difference-function zeros on the common domain, and locate candidate inputs with graphs or tables. Refine and verify numerical solutions to a stated precision, reject domain or continuity artifacts, and explain limits of sign-change searches and viewing windows. | Restrict all candidates to the common domain. Use graphs or tables and an appropriate refinement method to justify the stated input precision. Check original output agreement at a suitable tolerance. Require continuity for sign-change bracketing, investigate possible tangencies, and avoid unsupported claims that all roots have been found. |
| **Quadratic–quadratic systems** | For $y=ax^2+bx+c$ and $y=dx^2+ex+k$ on the real line, with $a,d\ne0$, equating outputs gives $(a-d)x^2+(b-e)x+(c-k)=0$. Coefficient cancellation can leave a quadratic, linear equation, constant contradiction, or identity. Distinct quadratic function graphs have at most two real intersections; identical formulas share the whole graph. This count concerns quadratic functions, not arbitrary quadratic relations. | Solve systems of two quadratic functions by equating outputs, simplifying coefficients, and recovering all intersection coordinates. Classify the simplified equation and distinguish identical graphs from systems with zero, one, or two real intersections. | Combine coefficients before choosing a solution method. Solve the actual reduced equation and recover every corresponding output. Distinguish an identity from a contradiction and justify the finite count for distinct graphs. Verify all pairs and restrict the count claim to quadratic functions of the same input. |
