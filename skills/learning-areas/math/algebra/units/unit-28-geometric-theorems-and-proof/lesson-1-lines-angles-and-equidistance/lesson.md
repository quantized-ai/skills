# Lesson 28.1: Lines, angles, and equidistance

Angle addition and congruent triangles support line-angle theorems and an equidistance locus.

## Learning objectives

- Prove and apply vertical-angle and parallel-transversal relationships and their parallelism converses.
- Prove both directions of the perpendicular-bisector theorem.

## Proficiency criteria

- Separate hypotheses of parallelism from conclusions and justify angle equalities without assuming diagram appearance.
- Show that the locus consists of all and only points equidistant from the endpoints.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Vertical and transversal angles** | Vertical angles are congruent by supplementary-angle relationships. Parallel lines cut by a transversal give equal corresponding and alternate interior angles and supplementary same-side interior angles; appropriate converses establish parallelism. Intersecting lines need not satisfy these parallel-line relations. | Prove and apply vertical-angle and parallel-transversal theorems and use their converses to establish parallelism. | Use linear pairs and angle addition in vertical-angle arguments; cite or derive the relevant parallel postulate consequence and distinguish a theorem from its converse. |
| **Perpendicular-bisector locus** | For distinct endpoints, a point is equidistant from them if and only if it lies on the perpendicular bisector of their segment. Congruent right triangles prove the forward distance property; congruent triangles or midpoint reasoning establish the converse, including a point at the midpoint. | Prove both directions of the perpendicular-bisector theorem and apply the equidistance characterization. | Handle points on the bisector and equidistant points separately; include the midpoint case and identify why the line is perpendicular and bisects the segment. |
