# Lesson 28.3: Triangle centers

Special lines and segments of a triangle define centers with different geometric meanings.

## Learning objectives

- Prove concurrence of medians and characterize the centroid.
- Establish perpendicular-bisector and angle-bisector concurrence through equidistance.

## Proficiency criteria

- Distinguish medians, altitudes, angle bisectors, and perpendicular bisectors.
- Give arguments that establish a common point for all three relevant lines, rather than merely showing that two intersect.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Medians and centroid** | A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of its opposite side. The three medians concur at the centroid, which divides each median in a \(2:1\) ratio measured from the vertex. A midpoint/parallel argument or coordinate argument establishes concurrence and the ratio. | Prove that the medians of a triangle concur and use the centroid division ratio. | Establish that the same point lies on all three medians and place the longer subsegment next to the vertex. |
| **Circumcenter and incenter** | Perpendicular bisectors of a triangle meet at a point equidistant from its vertices, the circumcenter. Angle bisectors meet at an interior point equidistant from the side lines, the incenter; distance to a line is perpendicular distance. The angle-bisector equidistance theorem and its converse follow from right-triangle congruence. An altitude passes through a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side line and is distinct from these constructions. | Prove perpendicular-bisector and angle-bisector concurrence and identify the corresponding circumcenter and incenter. | Use transitivity of equal distances to establish the third bisector, distinguish distance to vertices from distance to sides, and allow the circumcenter outside an obtuse triangle. |
