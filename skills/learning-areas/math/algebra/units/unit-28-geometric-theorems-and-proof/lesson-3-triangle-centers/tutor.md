## Concept teaching plans

### Medians and centroid

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** Median length 12: distance vertex to centroid?

**Private diagnostic key:** 8; centroid to midpoint 4.

**Teach in this order:** Define median by midpoint; calculate centroid as coordinate average; express position along each median; distinguish median from altitude.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** For A=(0,0),B=(6,0),C=(0,9), G=(2,3). Midpoint BC=(3,4.5); G is 2/3 of the way from A to it. Analogous coordinate averaging places G on the other medians, proving concurrence and the 2:1 ratio.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If ratio is reversed, ask which segment is measured from vertex; next mark the 2/3 point from A.

**Practice progression:** Median identification → ratio length → general coordinate concurrence proof. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Midpoints;2:1 from vertex; all three medians; inside location; no altitude/bisector confusion. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Establish that the same point lies on all three medians and place the longer subsegment next to the vertex.

### Circumcenter and incenter

**Diagnostic — ask and wait:** Which center is equidistant from vertices: incenter or circumcenter?

**Private diagnostic key:** circumcenter.

**Teach in this order:** Construct two defining bisectors; use transitivity of equidistance for the third; measure distances to vertices versus perpendicular distances to side lines.

**Distinct worked model — reveal in steps:** Triangle (0,0),(6,0),(0,8) has circumcenter (3,4), the hypotenuse midpoint, radius 5. Its incenter (2,2) has perpendicular distance 2 from both axes and from line 4x+3y=24. Same triangle, different centers and radii.

**Misconception response and hint ladder:** If vertex distance is used for inradius, ask what the incircle touches; next drop a perpendicular to a side.

**Practice progression:** Center identification → construction/exact example → acute/right/obtuse circumcenter locations and locus proof. Change a meaningful case or representation before increasing arithmetic size. Reuse the student's error as the focus of a new task, not by repeating an exposed answer.

**Assessment case checklist:** Perpendicular versus angle bisectors; concurrency; vertex/side-line distance; interior incenter; altitude distinct. Select independent tasks that collectively cover these cases and the curriculum row's full proficiency; a short session may leave named cases unassessed.

**Curriculum evidence contract:** Use transitivity of equal distances to establish the third bisector, distinguish distance to vertices from distance to sides, and allow the circumcenter outside an obtuse triangle.

## Extended private calibration

**Prompt:** For A=(0,0), B=(6,0), C=(0,6), find centroid, circumcenter and incenter; distinguish their defining distances.

**Private worked key:** Centroid G=(2,2), dividing the median from A to (3,3) in ratio 2:1. Circumcenter O=(3,3) is the hypotenuse midpoint, equidistant $3\sqrt2$ from all vertices. Inradius $r=(6+6-6\sqrt2)/2=6-3\sqrt2$, incenter I=(r,r), equidistant from all three side lines. Vertex distances define circumcenter; perpendicular side distances define incenter.

This previously checked composite example can connect concepts after instruction. Split it into manageable turns; it does not replace the distinct diagnostic and worked model for each concept.

## Additional worked coverage

For a general triangle with position vectors A,B,C, let M=(B+C)/2. The point G=(A+B+C)/3 satisfies G=A+(2/3)(M−A); the same calculation for each vertex puts G on all three medians and proves the 2:1 ratio. For circumcenter, equidistance from A,B and B,C implies equidistance from A,C, giving the third perpendicular bisector; for incenter use the internal angle-bisector equal-perpendicular-distance property to obtain equal distances to all three sides.

Use this reasoning as instruction or private calibration. Generate a fresh independent counterpart after exposure; this is not a fixed reassessment task.

## Decision rehearsal and fading

**Use the distance target to choose the center.** In triangle $A=(0,0)$, $B=(8,0)$, $C=(0,6)$, the circumcenter is $(4,3)$, at distance 5 from every vertex. The incenter is $(2,2)$: its distances to the axes are 2, and to line $3x+4y=24$ the perpendicular distance is $(24-6-8)/5=2$. The centroid is $(8/3,2)$, located two-thirds of the way from A to the midpoint $(4,3)$ of BC. None of these definitions is interchangeable.

If the learner uses a vertex distance as inradius, ask what an incircle touches. Next draw a perpendicular from the proposed center to a side; work the distance to one axis and leave the other checks. Fade to identifying which construction—median, angle bisector, or perpendicular bisector—answers each distance question without coordinates. Calculating these centers does not by itself establish the separate concurrence proofs.

## Additional teaching and execution activities

### Concurrence arguments, not only center calculations

For arbitrary vertices A,B,C viewed as coordinate pairs, set $G=(A+B+C)/3$. The midpoint opposite A is $M=(B+C)/2$; then $G=A+\frac23(M-A)$. The same formula with vertices permuted places the identical G on all three medians, with vertex-to-G twice G-to-midpoint. This establishes concurrence and direction of the ratio, beyond checking one median numerically.

Let O be the intersection of perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC in a nondegenerate triangle. Then OA=OB and OB=OC, so OA=OC. The locus converse puts O on the third perpendicular bisector. The location may be inside, on the hypotenuse, or outside for acute, right or obtuse triangles respectively; never force all circumcenters inside.

For the angle-bisector locus, take P strictly inside an angle and drop perpendiculars PU,PV to its side lines. If P lies on the internal bisector, the right triangles share hypotenuse AP and have equal angles at A, so AAS gives PU=PV. Conversely, if PU=PV, hypotenuse AP is common and HL gives equal angles at A. The interior condition selects the internal rather than external bisector.

Now intersect two internal bisectors of triangle ABC at I. Its distances to AB and AC agree, and its distances to AB and BC agree; transitivity gives equal distances to AC and BC. The interior locus converse puts I on the third internal bisector. Ask the student to identify where 'interior' and 'perpendicular distance' were used, and why merely drawing two bisectors does not by itself prove the third passes through I.
