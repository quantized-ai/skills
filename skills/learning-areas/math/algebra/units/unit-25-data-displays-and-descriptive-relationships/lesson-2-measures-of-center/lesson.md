# Lesson 25.2: Measures of center

A measure of center summarizes a distribution’s location but may respond differently to skewness or extreme observations.

## Learning objectives

- Calculate and interpret means, medians, modes, and weighted means.
- Explain the effect of unusual values on alternative center summaries.

## Proficiency criteria

- Use all observations with their multiplicities and sort data before finding the median.
- Keep the units of the observations and justify the chosen typical-value summary.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Arithmetic and weighted means** | For \(n>0\), \(\bar x=(\sum x_i)/n\). The mean is a balance point whose signed deviations sum to zero. A frequency-weighted mean is \((\sum w_ix_i)/(\sum w_i)\) for nonnegative weights with positive total. Combining group means requires weighting by their group sizes; an unweighted mean of means is generally inappropriate. | Calculate and interpret arithmetic and frequency-weighted means, including a combined mean from group sizes and means. | Use the correct total count or weight, include repeated values, retain the data units, and distinguish a mean from a possible individual observation. |
| **Median and sensitivity to extremes** | The median is the middle ordered value for odd sample size and the mean of the two middle ordered values for even sample size. It splits the ordered observations into halves, allowing ties. Unlike the mean, its value is resistant to the magnitudes of a small number of extreme observations. A mode is a most frequent value and need not be unique. | Calculate mean, median, and modes and explain their different responses to skewness, repeated values, and extreme observations. | Order the data, use the correct middle positions, identify all most-frequent values under a stated convention for equal frequencies, and justify a center summary in context rather than assume one measure is always best. |
