# Lesson 25.1: Statistical variables and data displays

A statistical question anticipates variability, and a useful display preserves the meaning and type of the observed variable.

## Learning objectives

- Distinguish statistical questions and quantitative or categorical data.
- Construct and interpret frequency tables, dot plots, and histograms with informative scales.

## Proficiency criteria

- State the observational unit, variable, units, and number of observations.
- Represent every observation once and distinguish category counts from quantitative intervals.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Statistical questions and variable types** | A statistical question anticipates variation across observations. An observational unit is the entity measured; a variable is its recorded characteristic. Quantitative variables have numerical magnitude and may be discrete counts or continuous measurements. Categorical labels classify observations even when encoded as numbers; averaging arbitrary category codes has no numerical meaning. | Identify statistical questions, observational units, and variable types and describe the context and units of collected data. | Explain the expected variability, distinguish numerical labels from measurements, and report what each observation represents. |
| **Counts, frequencies, and categorical displays** | A frequency counts observations in a category or value; relative frequency divides by the total nonzero count. A frequency table accounts for every observation using mutually exclusive categories or bins. Bar displays separate categories; numerical histograms instead represent adjacent intervals along a numerical scale. Display choices can distort comparisons through missing labels or misleading scales. | Construct frequency and relative-frequency summaries and select displays appropriate to categorical and quantitative variables. | Reconcile totals, label denominators and axes, and distinguish category bars from numerical intervals without treating code values as measurements. |
| **Dot plots and histograms** | A dot plot preserves individual quantitative values and multiplicities. A histogram groups values into nonoverlapping intervals, with an explicit endpoint convention; equal-width bins allow heights to represent counts or relative frequencies. Bin choice can change apparent shape without changing data. Both displays reveal clusters, gaps, modes, and unusual observations. | Construct and interpret dot plots and equal-width histograms and describe how binning affects visible distribution features. | Place each observation exactly once, use a consistent bin-boundary rule, label numerical units and frequency scales, and distinguish visible aggregate counts from unrecoverable exact values. |
