# Lesson 25.7: Residuals, correlation, and causal claims

Residual analysis and correlation assess different aspects of linear fit, while study context limits the conclusions an association supports.

## Learning objectives

- Analyze residuals to judge linear-model suitability.
- Compute and interpret correlation and distinguish association from causation.

## Proficiency criteria

- Use observed minus predicted values and interpret patterns rather than residual size alone.
- Avoid treating correlation as a slope, a nonlinear-association measure, or evidence of causation.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Residuals and model fit** | A residual is \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\), the observed response minus the model prediction at the same input. A residual plot against input or predicted value reveals patterns. A roughly patternless spread around zero supports a linear form, whereas systematic curvature suggests missing structure; changing spread indicates unequal prediction variability. Small residuals over observed data do not guarantee reliable extrapolation. | Calculate and plot residuals and use their signs, magnitudes, and patterns to assess a linear model. | Match each prediction to its observation, preserve residual sign and units, and explain why visible patterns or unusual residuals affect confidence in the model. |
| **Correlation coefficient** | For paired numerical data with nonzero variation in both variables, Pearson correlation \(r\) is a unitless number in \([-1,1]\) measuring direction and strength of linear association. Its magnitude near one indicates a strong linear pattern; a value near zero does not rule out nonlinear association. Outliers can strongly alter it, and it is not the fitted slope. | Compute correlation with technology and interpret its direction, linear strength, conditions of definition, and sensitivity to unusual observations. | Associate the sign with direction and magnitude with linear strength, examine the scatter plot alongside the coefficient, and distinguish zero variation from a defined correlation of zero. |
| **Association and causation** | Observed association may result from a causal effect, reverse influence, a lurking variable, selection effects, or chance. A fitted line or strong correlation alone cannot distinguish these explanations. The data collection process and study design determine which claims are justified; a plausible mechanism alone does not establish causation. | Distinguish associational from causal claims and identify plausible alternative explanations for an observed relationship. | State what the data establish, name a relevant potential confounding or selection explanation, and identify the need for study-design evidence before asserting causation. |
