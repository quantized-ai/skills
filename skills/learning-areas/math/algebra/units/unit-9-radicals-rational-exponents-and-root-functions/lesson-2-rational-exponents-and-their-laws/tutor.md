**Prompt:** A learner rewrites x^(1/3) as the principal sixth root of x² because 1/3=2/6. Does that preserve values for negative x?

**Private reasoning key:** No. At x=−8 the original real cube root is −2, while the principal sixth root of 64 is 2. Reduce the exponent before applying the real-power convention; an unreduced radical rewrite can select a different sign or domain.

Ask what changed between the original claim and the repaired explanation. If the response is only a guessed answer, use the corresponding concept’s targeted cue below; after feedback, collect a new independent attempt.

## Concept guidance

### Rational exponents as roots

Curriculum reference: **Rational exponents as roots** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Evaluate $(-8)^{2/3}$ using the reduced-exponent convention.

**Agent key:** The real cube root is -2 and its square is 4.

**Worked example:** Evaluate $16^{-3/4}$ and discuss $0^{-1/2}$.

**Worked reasoning:** $16^{-3/4}=1/(2^3)=1/8$; the zero base with a negative exponent is undefined.


#### Teaching sequence

Reduce the rational exponent m/n first and interpret the positive denominator n as a root index. For real negative bases the reduced denominator must be odd; zero requires separate treatment when the exponent is zero or negative. Negative exponents introduce reciprocals only where the base-root value is nonzero. Explain why an unreduced radical rewrite can change apparent domains.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Is the exponent reduced, and does it demand a reciprocal?

If 16^(−3/4) becomes negative, distinguish reciprocal from sign. If (−8)^(2/3) is rejected because of an even numerator, inspect denominator parity after reduction. If zero to a negative exponent is allowed, show the zero divisor.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Use positive bases first, then reduced fractions, negative bases with odd/even denominators and zero exceptions. Compare notation with an explicitly supplied radical form.

#### Assessment evidence

Require exponent reduction, correct root/power interpretation, reciprocal handling and the original real domain. Do not assert a convention for 0⁰ without the task defining one.

### Exponent laws with stated hypotheses

Curriculum reference: **Exponent laws with stated hypotheses** in the [curriculum concept table](lesson.md#concepts). Read that row’s Content, Learning Objectives, and Proficiency criteria before using these activities.

#### Calibration and worked reasoning

**Diagnostic prompt:** Simplify $x^{1/3}x^{2/3}$ for $x>0$.

**Agent key:** Add exponents to get x under the stated positive-base hypothesis.

**Worked example:** Why is $(x^2)^{1/2}=x$ not valid for every real x?

**Worked reasoning:** The left side is $\lvert x\rvert$. At x=-2 it is 2, so unrestricted power-of-power simplification fails.


#### Teaching sequence

Establish exponent laws under a stated positive-base hypothesis, where roots and real powers behave consistently. Explain multiplication, division and power-of-power using the exponent relationships while retaining nonzero requirements for division. Then test whether an unrestricted extension survives a negative or zero input; √(x²)=|x| exposes one important failure.

#### Respond to student reasoning

**First hint:** Which hypotheses allow the exponent law?

If a law is applied before checking its hypotheses, identify the base and allowed set. If x^(1/2)+x^(1/2) is combined by adding exponents, distinguish addition from multiplication. If domains change after simplification, record the original conditions.

Give only the cue relevant to the observed error; wait for a revision before showing a worked step. Record any mathematical help as assistance.

#### Practice progression

Begin with valid positive-base simplifications, then ask for an explicit counterexample to an overextended law and a repaired domain statement.

#### Assessment evidence

Assess justified law use, operation distinction, original-domain preservation and a valid counterexample. Numerical success at one positive input is not evidence for a negative-base claim.
