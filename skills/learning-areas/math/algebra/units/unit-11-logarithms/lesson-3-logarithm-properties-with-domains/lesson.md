# Lesson 11.3: Logarithm properties with domains

Logarithm properties follow from exponential identities but carry argument restrictions. Expansion or condensation can change a formula’s natural domain, especially when products or even powers conceal negative factors.

## Learning objectives

- Derive product and quotient logarithm identities and apply product, quotient, and power properties under valid hypotheses; distinguish them from invalid sum rules.
- Compare original and transformed domains and use absolute values or explicit restrictions to state valid identities.

## Learning outcome

The student can transform logarithmic expressions while stating exactly where the transformed and original expressions agree.

## Proficiency criteria

- Justify product and quotient identities using exponent laws with a common valid base.
- Check positivity of each separate logarithm argument before expansion or condensation.
- Apply the real power rule to positive quantities and use absolute values to preserve negative nonzero inputs when expanding even powers.
- State any domain enlargement or loss and the domain of agreement; reject a general logarithm-of-a-sum rule.

## Concepts

| Concept Title | Content | Learning Objectives | Proficiency criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Product and quotient properties** | For $b>0$, $b\ne1$, and $M,N>0$, $\log_b(MN)=\log_bM+\log_bN$ and $\log_b(M/N)=\log_bM-\log_bN$, derived from exponent product and quotient laws. A positive product or quotient alone does not make its separate factors valid logarithm arguments. Expansion may lose negative-factor branches; condensation may give a formula with a larger natural domain. Equivalence must retain original restrictions and be claimed only where both expressions are defined, unless a valid rewrite restores the full original domain. | Derive and use product and quotient logarithm identities for positive arguments in a common valid base. Compare domains when expanding or condensing logarithms and state the precise domain of equivalence. | Justify both identities through exponent laws. Check positivity of separate arguments instead of only their product or quotient. Identify domain losses or enlargements and retain original restrictions when stating equivalence. |
| **Power properties and absolute values** | For a valid logarithm base, $M>0$, and real $r$, $\log_b(M^r)=r\log_bM$. For a positive integer $n$ and real $M\ne0$, $\log_b(M^{2n})=2n\log_b\lvert M\rvert$ preserves the full domain; omitting absolute values restricts it to $M>0$. Expansion and condensation retain original domain restrictions. There is no general identity that replaces a logarithm of a sum with a sum of logarithms. | Expand and condense powers of positive logarithmic arguments and distinguish valid power identities from invalid sum rules. Use absolute values or explicit restrictions for even powers and compare original and transformed domains. | Apply the real power rule with its positive-argument hypothesis. Preserve negative nonzero inputs in even-power expansions using absolute values, or state a restricted domain of agreement. Identify domain changes and reject a general sum rule. |
