# Unit 11: Logarithms

Logarithmic notation, identities, graphs, and equations invert exponential relationships and quantify growth or decay durations.

## Main learning objectives

- Convert between logarithmic and exponential statements, justifying valid bases and argument restrictions. Evaluate logarithms in bases 2, 10, and $e$ and check exact or approximate values against known powers.
- Construct logarithmic parent graphs by reflecting exponential graphs and describe their features and end behavior. Graph transformations of logarithms using mapped points and determine domains, ranges, asymptotes, intercepts, and end behavior.
- Derive product and quotient logarithm identities and apply product, quotient, and power properties under valid hypotheses; distinguish them from invalid sum rules. Compare original and transformed domains and use absolute values or explicit restrictions to state valid identities.
- Derive and apply change of base with valid argument and base conditions, checking numerical approximations. Simplify matching exponential-logarithmic compositions while preserving their original domains.
- Solve exponential equations with linear exponents using logarithms and formulate and solve logarithmic equations using exponential conversion or equality of positive arguments. Determine existence and preserve original restrictions while validating exact and approximate solutions, contextual units, and rejected candidates.
- Calculate doubling times and half-lives with model units and explain their independence from a positive initial amount. Formulate and solve exponential target equations, then interpret thresholds under time-domain and observation constraints.

## Concepts and topics

Logarithms as exponents; Base 2, common logarithms, and natural logarithms; Parent logarithms and reflection; Transformations of logarithmic graphs; Product and quotient properties; Power properties and absolute values; Change-of-base formula; Inverse identities and their domains; Solving exponential equations with logarithms; Solving logarithmic equations and rejecting invalid roots; Doubling time and half-life; Formulating and validating logarithmic solutions.

## Lessons

- [11.1: Definition and basic evaluation](lesson-1-definition-and-basic-evaluation/lesson.md) — A logarithm names the exponent that produces a given positive argument. Definition-based reasoning supports exact values, calculator approximations, and the distinction between an invalid argument and a negative logarithm.
- [11.2: Logarithmic graphs](lesson-2-logarithmic-graphs/lesson.md) — Logarithmic graphs reflect exponential input-output pairs across the diagonal. Their transformed domains and asymptotes come from positive arguments rather than from memorized shifts alone.
- [11.3: Logarithm properties with domains](lesson-3-logarithm-properties-with-domains/lesson.md) — Logarithm properties follow from exponential identities but carry argument restrictions. Expansion or condensation can change a formula’s natural domain, especially when products or even powers conceal negative factors.
- [11.4: Change of base and inverse identities](lesson-4-change-of-base-and-inverse-identities/lesson.md) — Change of base permits numerical evaluation, while inverse identities explain which operations undo each other. Both require attention to valid bases and to the order-dependent domains of the compositions.
- [11.5: Exponential and logarithmic equations](lesson-5-exponential-and-logarithmic-equations/lesson.md) — Logarithms make general exponential equations solvable, and exponential conversion solves logarithmic equations. Quantitative relationships can be formulated as logarithmic equations with defined variables and units. The original positive-argument conditions remain decisive when algebra produces multiple or invalid candidates.
- [11.6: Duration and reasonableness](lesson-6-duration-and-reasonableness/lesson.md) — Logarithmic solutions describe durations and threshold crossings in exponential models. Interpretation separates a continuous crossing time from an allowed observation time and explains which parameters affect doubling or halving periods.
